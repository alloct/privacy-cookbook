"I have nothing to hide" is the most common response to privacy concerns. It
misunderstands what data collection does with ordinary, innocent information.

## Data outlives its context

Information collected today is judged by the standards of an unknown future.
Location history from a period when visiting a particular clinic, attending a
particular meeting, or living in a particular neighborhood was unremarkable
can become sensitive when laws, governments, or your own circumstances change.
You cannot delete data you no longer control, and data brokers resell it
indefinitely.

## You don't get to decide what's incriminating

Aggregated data supports inferences you never consented to. Purchase history
predicts pregnancy. Location data reveals religion (weekly visits to a place
of worship), health conditions (repeated visits to a specialist), and
relationships (two phones that spend every night at the same address).
Insurance pricing, tenant screening, and employment screening increasingly
consume this kind of derived data. The question is not whether you have
something to hide, but whether strangers should get to draw conclusions about
you from data you didn't know you were producing.

## Breaches happen to everyone

Every organization that stores your data is a future breach you have no
control over. The 2017 Equifax breach exposed Social Security numbers and
birth dates for roughly 147 million people who never chose to be Equifax
customers.[^equifax] Minimizing what is collected in the first place is the
only defense that works retroactively.

[^equifax]: Federal Trade Commission, [Equifax Data Breach Settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/enforcement/refunds/equifax-data-breach-settlement).

## Privacy is a collective good

Even if you personally accept surveillance, your contacts did not. Uploading
your address book exposes people who never agreed to it. Using
end-to-end-encrypted messaging protects the person on the other end of the
conversation, not just you. Normalizing encryption also protects the people
who need it most (journalists, abuse survivors, activists) because tools
used only by people with "something to hide" paint a target on them.

## It is not all-or-nothing

The goal is not to disappear. The goal is to stop leaking data you get nothing
in return for. Most of the measures in this cookbook cost minutes and remove
entire categories of exposure. Start with [threat
modeling](/start-here/threat-modeling/) to figure out which ones are worth it
for you.
