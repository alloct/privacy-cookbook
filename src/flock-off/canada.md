Flock Safety has told reporters that it has no partnerships with Canadian
municipalities, law enforcement agencies, or airports. Treat sightings of
"Flock cameras" in Canada with scepticism until an agency confirms it.

Plate readers themselves are another matter. They have been in use for years and
are expanding, supplied by other vendors, with far less public documentation than
their American equivalents.

## What is actually deployed

The visible pattern is a mix of vehicle-mounted readers on patrol cars and fixed
units at selected locations. Toronto Police began rolling out Axon plate readers
on cruisers in 2023 and now has front-facing units on hundreds of cars, along
with a small number of fixed Genetec cameras. Several Ontario services including
Hamilton, Peel, York, Waterloo, Sudbury, and the Ontario Provincial Police
operate the technology in vehicles or on roadways, and municipalities have folded
readers into expanded CCTV programmes.

Two features of the Canadian picture matter for research. Deployment is
fragmented across vendors and agencies rather than consolidated in one national
network, so there is no single audit artifact to request. And retention rules
vary widely between jurisdictions, with reported periods ranging from 72 hours to
a full month, which means the retention question has a real answer worth getting
in writing for your own area.

## Where they are mapped

[Panopti.ca](https://panopti.ca) is the Canadian counterpart to DeFlock, started
in 2025, with several hundred cameras plotted so far and most of them in Ontario.
Coverage is early, which means contributions are unusually valuable right now.

[DeFlock](https://deflock.org) renders from OpenStreetMap, so Canadian cameras
tagged in [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org) appear there too. The
tagging scheme is international. Adding a camera in Canada uses the same tags as
adding one in Ohio, and it improves every map built on that data.

## Which law applies

Getting records depends on identifying the right level of government, because
Canada has no single access statute:

- **Federal bodies**, including the RCMP, fall under the Access to Information
  Act, with privacy questions under the Privacy Act. The
  [Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada](https://www.priv.gc.ca/) has
  jurisdiction.
- **Provincial police and ministries** fall under provincial access legislation,
  such as Ontario's FIPPA or British Columbia's FIPPA.
- **Municipal police services** in Ontario fall under MFIPPA, and other
  provinces have equivalents. This is usually the right route for a city police
  department.
- **Provincial privacy regulators** handle complaints and sometimes publish
  investigation reports that are more informative than anything you would get
  from a request. Ontario's
  [Information and Privacy Commissioner](https://www.ipc.on.ca/) is the most
  active on surveillance technology.

Police services boards and municipal councils meet in public and publish
agendas. In practice that is where plate reader programmes are approved,
expanded, and renewed, and it is a far more productive place to ask questions
than a records request that takes three months.

## What to ask for

Canadian requests need to be more specific than American ones, because there is
no standard vendor report to name. Useful items:

- The vendor and model in use, and how many units, fixed and vehicle-mounted.
- The retention period for reads that do not match an alert, and the retention
  period for those that do.
- Who is authorised to query the data, whether a reason must be recorded, and
  whether the system produces an audit log at all. If it does not, that is
  itself the story.
- Any privacy impact assessment prepared before deployment.
- The policy on sharing with other agencies, and specifically with agencies
  outside Canada.
- Where the data is physically stored, and by whom.

Those last two are the sharpest questions available in Canada. American
reporting has established that plate reader data flows between agencies far
beyond the department that bought the cameras, including to federal immigration
authorities with no contract of their own. A Canadian force using an American
vendor with American cloud infrastructure should be able to explain precisely
what prevents the same thing happening with Canadian data, and the answer
belongs in public.
