Automated licence plate readers are the most widely deployed form of mass
surveillance that ordinary people never agreed to and mostly cannot see. This
section collects the tools, datasets, and records processes that make them
visible, organised by country, because the technology, the vendor, and the law
are different depending on where you drive.

The section is named after Flock Safety, the American company whose cameras
have become shorthand for this whole category. The scope is wider than one
vendor, for a reason explained below.

::: danger Responsibility

so does the local temper about it. If you are unsure how any of this applies to
you, or you are already in contact with law enforcement about it, get advice
from a lawyer in your jurisdiction rather than from a website.

## What these systems actually do

A licence plate reader photographs every passing vehicle, extracts the plate,
and records it with a timestamp and a location. Modern systems also record
vehicle make, colour, body type, and distinguishing features such as roof racks
or damage, which means they can be searched by description even when no plate
is legible.

Three properties make a networked reader different in kind from a speed camera
rather than different in degree.

It is retroactive. The record exists before anyone suspects you of anything, so
a query run today can reconstruct where a vehicle has been for as long as the
data is retained.

It is networked. An agency that installs cameras can usually share access with
other agencies, and in the United States that sharing has extended nationally,
which means a camera bought by a small town becomes queryable by people who
have never been accountable to that town.

It is searched by individuals. Every result comes from a person typing a plate
and a reason, and in the American system there are roughly 140,000 people with
that ability, a figure Flock itself gave the Washington Post while arguing that
documented abuse involved only a small share of them.

## The search log is the accountability surface

The cameras are the visible part and the least interesting. What matters is the
record of who ran which query, when, against how many networks, and why.

That record exists because the vendor sells it as a compliance feature, which
means it is often obtainable through ordinary public records law. It is how
journalists established that federal immigration enforcement was reaching Flock
data through local police departments without holding a contract, and how
several police officers were caught using the system to track partners and
ex-partners. In at least one case, the victims found the searches themselves by
looking up their own plates in a public database of released audit logs.

That is the practical shape of this work. Map the cameras to know what exists,
then request the logs to find out what it is being used for.

## A note on scope

Flock is an American company and, as far as any public evidence shows, an
American deployment. It has said it has no partnerships with Canadian
municipalities, police services, or airports, and there is no confirmed
deployment by any UK police force. Claims that "Flock cameras" have appeared on
a street outside the United States are usually a mix-up, sometimes with an
unrelated British insurance company of the same name.

That does not mean anyone else is unwatched. It means the system you live under
is probably built by a different vendor, or run directly by the police, under a
different legal framework and with different paperwork attached. So the country
pages here cover the plate reader network you actually have, along with the
route to information about it, rather than pretending Flock is everywhere.

If your country is not covered yet, [Everywhere Else](/flock-off/other-countries/)
describes the general approach, and the
[research methods](/flock-off/methods/) page works anywhere.
